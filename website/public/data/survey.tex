\begin{abstract}
Knowledge graph exploration combines entity and relationship inspection with iterative filtering, query construction, and interpretation. This systematic review relates interaction, guidance, representations, backend access, scalability, and evaluation. Searches covered Scopus, the ACM Digital Library, OpenAlex, and IEEE Xplore through 29 August 2026. Eligible reports were English-language, peer-reviewed primary publications describing human exploration or specified guidance over semantic knowledge graphs. The frozen search contains 4,159 raw occurrences and 3,030 deduplicated records. Two human title--abstract reviewers excluded 2,061 records; 969 database reports and 22 distinct existing-collection reports entered retrieval. The historical baseline assessed 324 of 991 sought reports, including 242 and excluding 82. An author-authorized recovery extension adds 73 reports, producing a current corpus of 315 included reports: 437 assessed, 122 excluded, 543 not retrieved, and 11 awaiting assessment. Codex used scoped source extraction for the extension; additional independent human verification is not attested. Descriptive coding and narrative comparisons preserve report-specific designs, samples, measures, and limitations. Report counts do not establish independent systems or studies. Narrative synthesis uses no common quality score or statistical pooling. Navigation, facets, paths, and query construction support different tasks and expose different constraints. Guidance separates executable refinements, approximate candidates, ranking, personalization, and explanation. Usability ratings, qualitative observations, ranking experiments, execution benchmarks, and deployment statistics support different inferences. Requirements concern visible query scope, recoverable state, provenance, computation bounds, and evaluation under stated task and user conditions. The retrieval gap, incomplete search provenance, and undocumented independent duplicate full-text review limit coverage and comparative inference. The review was not registered and received no specific funding or support.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Knowledge graphs represent entities, their attributes, and typed relationships in a form that can be inspected and queried. Exploration begins when a user does not yet know which entities, relationships, or constraints will resolve an information need. A historian may follow people through institutions and life events; a researcher may refine a set of publications through topics and collaborators; an analyst may construct a query while learning the vocabulary of an unfamiliar dataset. In each case, useful information can change the next question. The evolving search described by \citet{bates1989} provides a useful frame for these changes. Work on exploratory search connects this process with understanding beyond an isolated query response \citep{marchionini2006,white2009}.

The graph supplies potential routes through the data, but the interface determines which routes a user can perceive and control. An entity browser exposes neighboring resources. A facet interface narrows a set using available attribute values. A visual query builder makes a graph pattern explicit. A relationship finder searches for paths between chosen entities. These operations can coexist, yet they impose different demands on users. Recognizing a displayed relationship is different from selecting the join scope of a query; identifying an empty result is different from recognizing an endpoint failure. An interface that conceals these distinctions can produce a plausible display while leaving its meaning unclear.

Early systems already linked graph inspection with query formulation. Haystack provided a platform for semantic applications, while Tabulator combined an outliner, linked views, query-by-example, and source information \citep{2003_Quan_Haystack_A,2006_Berners-Lee_Tabulator_Exploring}. The /facet browser addressed heterogeneous repositories through type-sensitive facets and multi-type queries \citep{2006_Hildebrand_facet_A}. Later reports describe relationship discovery, guided queries, graph summaries, reusable portals, and domain-specific recommendations. Their continued coexistence suggests that exploration is better understood as a collection of workflows than as a progression toward one universal interface.

Existing reviews cover visualization, Linked Data consumption, and query builders. A comparative review of exploration also needs to connect visible behavior with its computational basis and the evidence supporting it. A recommendation may be constrained by schema, generated from instances, ranked by query context, or adapted to a profile. A displayed edge may be a source assertion, a computed similarity, or an inferred relationship. A scalability claim may concern indexing, query execution, the number of stored triples, or the number of items rendered. These differences affect what a practitioner can reasonably expect when adopting a method.

This review examines exploration over graph-structured knowledge when a report describes a user-facing interaction or an identifiable guidance method in sufficient detail. Resource Description Framework (RDF) and Linked Data systems form a substantial part of the material, but RDF and SPARQL are not eligibility requirements. Property graphs and legacy typed semantic graphs can also meet the scope. Pure graph construction, ontology engineering, autonomous reasoning, and exact-answer systems enter the review only when the primary contribution supplies a concrete exploration or guidance method. This boundary allows method reports without a graphical interface while excluding backend work whose connection to human exploration is only prospective.

Five research questions organize the synthesis:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.},topsep=0.2em,itemsep=0.15em,parsep=0pt,midpenalty=10000]
\item Which interaction workflows support knowledge graph exploration, and what tasks and user roles do they address?
\item How do systems generate, constrain, rank, and present guidance, and how do query context and exploration history contribute?
\item How do data models, schema assumptions, preprocessing, and backend choices affect portability and interactive scalability?
\item What evidence supports usability, effectiveness, and efficiency under the reported evaluation conditions?
\item Which implementation and reproducibility limitations inform requirements for future exploration applications?
\end{enumerate}

The contribution is a framework for relating these questions at the level of reports and systems, together with a source-grounded comparison. Report identity and evaluation provenance are part of that comparison. A demonstration and a detailed paper about the same tool remain separate reports. Report counts are not interpreted as independent study or cohort counts. Intended users are kept separate from actual participants. Features implemented in the assessed version are distinguished from proposals. This gives the taxonomy an evidential basis and makes its practical implications inspectable.

\section{Review Method}
\subsection{Protocol, Scope, and Analytical Units}
The review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 reporting framework \citep{prisma2020}. It uses a descriptive and narrative synthesis because the reports investigate different tasks, user populations, datasets, outcomes, and versions. A statistical meta-analysis is not attempted. No common effect measure, pooled effect estimate, or statistical estimate of between-study heterogeneity is computed. Report-specific outcomes are retained in their original units. The review was not registered. The working protocol is recorded in the review workbook; prospective publication or preregistration is not claimed. Amendments on 5--6 October 2026 corrected the reviewer count and corpus accounting and documented the progression of unresolved title--abstract cases to full text. Criteria for grouping and retaining reports in narrative comparisons were documented on 6 October 2026.

Eligible primary reports must concern a semantic knowledge graph as an exploration object, describe interaction or guidance as a primary contribution, and provide enough methodological or implementation detail to identify the supported operation. The publication must meet the English-language and peer-reviewed publication criteria and fall within the 29 August 2026 cutoff. Conference and workshop papers are assessed through their actual publication track and available editorial documentation. A proceedings venue alone does not establish the review procedure of every track. Tutorials, contextual reviews, unreviewed preprints, abstract-only descriptions, and reports outside the topic are excluded from the primary synthesis. Their bibliographic roles remain distinguishable, so a contextual review can inform the introduction without becoming a new exploration system.

Four analytical units are retained. A \emph{record} is an indexed entry before report retrieval. A \emph{report} is an identifiable publication or assessed version. A \emph{system} is a named application or method family that can occur in several reports. A \emph{study} is a particular evaluation with its own design, participants, tasks, and measurements. Exact duplicate records are merged at the record stage; companion publications are linked rather than merged. A common system name does not prove that two reports share an evaluation cohort. Publication year, event year, deposit date, and software release date are recorded separately when they differ.

\subsection{Information Sources and Search}
The frozen search record comprises Scopus, the ACM Digital Library, OpenAlex, and IEEE Xplore. The selected formulation combines a graph-domain block with an exploration and interaction block. It includes RDF, SPARQL, Linked Data, knowledge graphs, property graphs, Cypher, and graph databases, alongside browsing, facets, navigation, relationship discovery, query construction, guidance, autocompletion, and visualization. The preserved portable expression and source adaptations are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:search}. Preliminary formulations are retained as calibration history rather than added to the final search count.

The primary sources yielded 1,021 Scopus records, 1,668 ACM hits, 1,279 OpenAlex records, and 191 occurrences across the IEEE subqueries. The raw total is 4,159. ACM export deduplication removed 28 within-source occurrences, and IEEE export deduplication removed nine, leaving 4,122 exported occurrences. Cross-source and reviewed near-title deduplication reduced these to 3,030 records. Thus, 1,129 duplicate occurrences were removed from the raw total, including both within-source and cross-source removal. The distinction matters: 4,122 minus 3,030 describes export-level reduction, whereas the PRISMA raw-identification total requires the 4,159 denominator.

The original ACM, OpenAlex, and IEEE export files were not available for this audit. Their source counts and within-source reductions are preserved in the search log and reconciled with the frozen consolidated corpus, but the original export-to-corpus transformations cannot be independently replayed. The seven exact submitted IEEE query strings were also not retained. The combined expression, partition description, and recorded block counts support only partial reproduction of that search.

An existing collection contributes a separate retrieval route. Sixty-one local copies were reconciled to 39 already represented reports and 22 distinct reports. This is a report-identity reconciliation, not evidence that a new database search found 22 records. The original identification dates and methods for these distinct reports are not established. Their contribution is therefore reported as an existing collection, with this provenance limitation retained. Checks conducted on 5--6 October 2026 verified identities, dates, sources, and contents of frozen records; they did not extend the literature cutoff or constitute a new topic search.

The search log records a supplementary Google Scholar search covering the first 200 relevance-ranked results on 29 August 2026. Its raw results export was unavailable, so the search record does not permit independent replay of that result set. The supplementary route is not added to the four-source corpus or treated as 200 eligible systems. Web of Science, DBLP, and backward citation searching were listed as planned sources, but their execution is not documented. They are therefore not described as completed searches.

The title--abstract stage has two confirmed independent human reviewers. Their recorded decisions and subsequent adjudication retain separate provenance. Full-text eligibility was reviewed by one person, as confirmed by the authors on 6 October 2026; independent duplicate full-text review was not performed. Following this stage, 2,061 records are excluded and 969 advance to report retrieval. Adding the 22 distinct existing-collection reports gives 991 reports sought. The frozen assessment covered 324 reports, of which 242 met the criteria and 82 were excluded with recorded reasons; 667 reports were not retrieved in that assessment snapshot. These figures distinguish retrieval, assessment, and inclusion. They do not establish independent full-text agreement or a count of independent evaluation studies. A subsequent public-source recovery pass retains dated request receipts and acquired copies in a separate ledger. Acquisition alone does not establish eligibility; recovered sources enter the synthesis only after full-text assessment and source-grounded extraction, with a synchronized revision of the selection register, catalogue, and analytical counts.

\subsection{Extraction and Evidence Appraisal}
Three people participated in data extraction, as confirmed by the authors on 6 October 2026. This count does not establish that each report was extracted independently by all three people. Per-report allocation, duplicate extraction, and the checking or disagreement-resolution procedure are not documented. Extraction covers title, authors, report identity, publication dates, graph object, implemented workflow, guidance mechanism, representations, backend, scalability, evaluation, and artifacts. Each substantive field carries a report locator. A complete paper is distinguished from a supplement, a proceedings container, or a truncated copy. An appendix containing algorithms does not establish that the corresponding main report was read. When a container includes several papers, evidence is limited to the pages of the requested report.

Evaluation extraction preserves design, participant count and background, task, comparator, measures, results, and limitations. Missing values remain unreported. A count of sessions, judgments, queries, devices, graph entities, or website visitors is not converted into a count of human participants. An earlier study cited by a new report retains its original identity. The reported evaluation details permit discussion of task relevance, participant selection, comparator suitability, allocation and learning effects, measure interpretation, uncertainty reporting, and materials availability. The appraisal procedure and responsibilities require author confirmation; no common numerical quality score is computed.

The six-dimensional framework uses multiple codes per report when warranted. Implemented, proposed, and future functionality remain distinct. Cross-cutting descriptors include domain, intended expertise, evaluated expertise, report genre, version, and availability. Narrative comparisons are restricted to compatible tasks and evidence types. The examples illustrate an operation, assumption, reported outcome, or limitation; their selection does not estimate how typical a system is. Missingness is reported explicitly rather than treated as feature absence. Comparisons summarize documented operations and source-specific outcomes; retrieval gaps and unresolved system-family denominators limit corpus-wide feature-prevalence claims.

\subsection{Grouping and Narrative Synthesis}
The included corpus supplies both the descriptive profile and the thematic discussion. The same eligibility criteria apply to every primary report. Backend and data access record the graph object, execution language, access route, and schema requirements. Scalability distinguishes measured behavior from strategies and source sizes. Evaluation records the kind of evidence and its conditions.

Cross-dimensional analysis is necessary because visible similarity can conceal different assumptions. A graph view backed by dereferenced documents has different freshness and failure behavior from a local indexed graph. Domain and user expertise are therefore descriptors across the six dimensions, not substitute explanations for these differences.

\section{Interaction Workflows and Representations}
\subsection{Entity Navigation and Relationship Discovery}
Entity navigation supports incremental inspection when users can identify a starting resource but have not fixed a query. Tabulator follows linked resources through an outliner and exposes alternative analytical views together with source information \citep{2006_Berners-Lee_Tabulator_Exploring}. LodLive uses a resource-centered graph view; LODmilla adds an editable exploration canvas, bounded relationship search, undo, and saved shared states \citep{2012_Camarda_LodLive_exploring,2014_AMA_LODmilla_Shared}. These operations preserve useful context, but the explored subgraph remains a selection from a larger source. A visible neighborhood does not establish that all relevant relationships have been retrieved.

RelFinder makes the relationship itself the search object. Users select entities, disambiguate labels, and inspect directed paths obtained through iterative SPARQL searches \citep{2009_Heim_RelFinder_Revealing}. Path length, direction, and search bounds determine which relationships appear. This is distinct from simply expanding neighboring nodes: a path-search interface must communicate its search scope and the meaning of an absent path. Computed association graphs also require explicit semantics. Semantic Wonder Cloud combines exploration with similarity-based connections; these connections must be interpreted as computed relationships rather than automatically as assertions present in DBpedia \citep{V3-02469}.

\subsection{Facets, Sets, and Coordinated Views}
Facets make a set of entities and its available refinements visible. The /facet browser generates facets appropriate to different entity types and supports movement between related types \citep{2006_Hildebrand_facet_A}. gFacet represents filtering graphically; its 2008 study indicates that prior facet knowledge affected success on some music tasks \citep{2008_Heim_gFacet_A}. Explorator instead exposes direct manipulation and set operations over RDF resources \citep{2009_Araujo_Experimenting_with}. These approaches share iterative narrowing but differ in how users recognize the current set and the effect of a new relation.

Rhizomer combines facets with pivoting, retaining previous constraints when a related resource type becomes the focus \citep{V3-01105}. Sampo-UI separates faceted search from analytical perspectives such as maps, timelines, and statistical views, with entity landing pages and reusable portal components \citep{2022_Ikkala_Sampo-UI_A}. S-Paths characterizes semantic paths and uses coordinated set-based views to inspect connected data \citep{2021_Destandau_S-Paths_Set-based}. To preserve continuity, a change of type or view should reveal whether the selected population, relation direction, or aggregation unit has changed.

Domain-specific views can express distinctions that a general node--link layout obscures. GeoChange-Viz coordinates territorial maps, change descriptions, and a change graph; its interpretation depends on explicit geographic-change semantics \citep{V3-01218}. GlyCosmos exposes typed biological filters and a Gene Ontology hierarchy \citep{V3-01242}. A specialized representation can support a concrete domain task, but a described example does not establish general usability across domains.

\subsection{Query Construction and Reuse}
Visual query construction lets users formulate relationships and restrictions while inspecting their formal consequences. ViziQuer represents classes, attributes, links, optionality, aggregation, and subquery scope; the supported constructs depend on the assessed version \citep{2018_Cerans_ViziQuer_A,V3-01077}. QueryVOWL supplies a visual notation and a composition interface, reported in a detailed paper and a companion demonstration \citep{V3-02190,V3-02191}. KGVQL addresses bidirectional transformations between visual and textual query forms \citep{V3-01623}. These contributions address representation and expressiveness; correctness of the transformation remains distinct from a user's ability to formulate the intended question.

Textual authoring remains useful for experienced users. YASGUI combines editing, completion, endpoint selection, and result presentation \citep{2013_Rietveld_YASGUI_Not}. The lightweight-metadata editor assessed in the 2026 report extends this route with examples, contextual completion, and schema information \citep{2025_Emonet_A_User-Friendly}. PICASSO is a contextual example outside the included semantic-knowledge-graph corpus. Its results wall combines progressive visual reformulations, result streams, and exploration history over a collection of graphs \citep{2017_Huang_PICASSO_exploratory}. These interaction ideas inform the comparison without changing the recorded scope exclusion of V3-02098. Its graph-database setting does not establish Cypher support. Across these workflows, query reuse and state restoration deserve separate coding from the mere presence of a history list.

The recovered reports make the transition from browsing to querying more explicit. Smeagol lets users navigate concrete resources, select a subgraph and generalize nodes into variables; RDF Explorer combines pattern construction with constant-to-variable generalization and current-result feedback. The DBpedia Visualizer applies related operations through Android gestures, while KGVis allows transformations between query patterns and result graphs \citep{V3-02525,V3-02210,V3-00229,V3-01622}. The reusable state is therefore more than a displayed neighborhood: it includes which nodes are constants, which are variables and which edges constrain the answer. The DBpedia report describes filters, aggregates and autocomplete as future work, so the gesture-based workflow does not establish the expressiveness of a full SPARQL editor.

A similar-looking graph can represent different objects at successive stages. The visual data-schema library contracts classes and properties to produce configurable UML-like source-schema diagrams; initiating queries directly from those diagrams is future work. Schema-based ViziQuer uses a class tree to start queries, and the Wikidata extension displays a query pattern with readable entity labels and reverse SPARQL visualization \citep{V3-00138,V3-02393,V3-02757}. KG3D, in contrast, uses a three-dimensional entity view alongside query construction and tabular answers \citep{V3-01616}. Schema simplification, query notation and result exploration require separate representation roles even when they use nodes and links. Lossy schema abstraction also needs an explicit indication of what the view omits.

Query-by-example is not one uniform interaction. The GF framework uses a grid of classes, properties, conditions and selected output fields. CUTE accepts tabular example tuples; iQbees starts from an entity and updates semantic-aspect rankings through positive and negative feedback. SPARQL-QBE builds conjunctive constraints from positive and negative entities, while Von-QBE maps keyword input through an ontology schema and suggests fragment expansions \citep{V3-00165,V3-00742,V3-01566,V3-02185,V3-02018}. These mechanisms differ in whether an example specifies a table row, an entity, a graph fragment or a conceptual starting point. Their expressive limits and feedback semantics should be stated before treating the shared name as evidence of equivalent user control.

Views can also reorganize relationships without changing their role into source assertions. LDViz connects editable SPARQL queries and style sheets with coordinated MGExplorer views and exploration history. ARViz synchronizes scatter, chord and association-graph views of mined rules and provides links to the supporting publications \citep{V3-01178,V3-00411}. Stunning Doodle places embedding neighbors beside asserted knowledge-graph links, with distance-based shading and a neighbor list \citep{V3-02586}. These examples support preserving the distinction between returned facts, mined associations and embedding similarity throughout selection, explanation and export. Sparklis nested tables address a different presentation problem by using query-variable dependencies to group results and reduce repeated values; no measured usability gain is supplied for that extension \citep{V3-00261}.

Chem2Bio2RDF adds a domain-specific combination of facets, generated relationship paths and resource-level inspection. DrugBank entries can be narrowed and presented as tables, tiles, thumbnails or approval timelines; the ontology-driven link-path generator connects selected classes and produces SPARQL. A Cytoscape plugin exposes resource menus and related MEDLINE/PubMed literature \citep{V3-00576}. The report's biomedical examples and source-coverage comparisons establish concrete discovery operations, without a recruited-user study or an interaction-latency benchmark. The assessed author manuscript uses a title omitting ``Systems''; the published IEEE title, DOI and author sequence are retained separately, without substituting the related journal article.

Inference Variable proposes an encoding aligned with the question being investigated. In its time-oriented organizational example, subject and object capsules span occurrence-to-cessation intervals and relationship arcs show activity across those intervals; current and past attributes have distinct marks \citep{V3-01484}. This is a proposed representation and visual inferencing procedure over typed semantic relationships, without an established implemented interface, RDF serialization or graph-store backend. Its authored employee-selection example motivates cognitive-fit and load hypotheses, but supplies no participant trial or cognitive-load measurement. It therefore extends the representation comparison while leaving its usability benefit open.

\section{Guidance, Context, and Explanations}
\subsection{Valid Refinements and Candidate Generation}
Guidance first determines which actions are available. Sparklis constructs queries through a focus-based sequence of refinements, with controlled-language verbalization and suggestions derived from the current endpoint context \citep{2017_Ferre_Sparklis_An}. RDF Explorer combines a visual query with data-aware property suggestions \citep{2019_Vargas_RDF_Explorer}. A schema-compatible refinement need not return data; an instance-supported refinement may be valid only within the current partial results or endpoint snapshot. The scope of validity should therefore accompany the suggestion.

Approximation changes this contract. Fast Approximate Autocompletion for SPARQL query builders uses a local graph summary to reduce reliance on expensive exact endpoint operations \citep{2021_Parra_Fast_Approximate}. Relevance was assessed only for queries for which the public Wikidata Query Service (WDQS) returned results. Across that subset, median precision was 0.21: precision measures the fraction of FAAS suggestions also returned by the exact endpoint query. Recall ranged from 0.92 to 1.00, with median 1.00; the authors traced imperfect recall to updates in WDQS after the indexed dump. These are query-level suggestion measures. A fast candidate list is useful when users can recognize and recover from suggestions that do not yield results. Presenting approximate candidates as guaranteed nonempty refinements would conceal an observed limitation.

Templates and formal descriptions offer another entry point. ShEx2SPARQL translates Shape Expressions blueprints into query structures, including ASK, SELECT, CONSTRUCT, VALUES, and optional patterns \citep{V3-01110}. Its report supplies concrete generation rules and limitations, including unsupported recursion and imports; it does not provide a usability trial or execution benchmark. Petrova's entity-comparison proposal identifies commonalities and differences through conjunctive queries and formal ranking conditions \citep{V3-00633}. Such guidance methods can be in scope without an implemented graphical workflow, while their practical benefit remains untested.

Schema-derived entry patterns also have a distinct operational contract. The peer-reviewed ShEx2SPARQL report accepts a Shape Expressions schema, an output query type and optionally a resource URI, and generates SELECT or CONSTRUCT patterns for exploring shape-conforming resources. ASK supplies an exact compliance check and requires a URI; that capability alone does not establish exploration \citep{V3-02495}. Its prototype executes 292 Wikidata Entity-Schema CONSTRUCT queries: 187 initially receive HTTP 200, and 47 more succeed with a one-result limit. Fifty-six of the 234 successful responses are empty. Request success, nonempty answers and user usefulness are therefore separate outcomes. Recursive shapes and imports remain unsupported, and no graphical authoring or recruited-user trial is established.

\subsection{Ranking and Personalization}
Ranking orders available actions rather than proving their validity. VIIQ recommends edges and labels during graph-query formulation \citep{2015_Jayaram_VIIQ_auto-suggestion}. Graph-Query Suggestions applies language-model and pseudo-relevance-feedback methods to candidate expansions \citep{2020_Lissandrini_Graph-Query_Suggestions}. The latter's results depend on the information already present in the query. With a single exemplar entity, no evaluated method significantly outperformed random ranking; an edge or a larger query supplied context that improved the effectiveness of the best-ranked expansions. It would be misleading to generalize the larger-query result to every initial exemplar.

Personalization adds a user-specific source of context. The Linked Data browser with recommendations uses user interaction to guide subsequent resources \citep{2018_Durao_A_Linked}. FeedLens exposes related entity types through lenses tied to a curated publication feed \citep{V3-01126}. Grapevine2D allows users to inspect and alter topic weights and recommendation categories through sliders and carousels \citep{V3-00694}. These mechanisms differ in what a user can inspect and change. Explicit controls make preferences visible; inferred profiles require explanations of which observations produced the adaptation. A relevance score derived from a recommender's own model also requires care when it is reused as an evaluation outcome.

\subsection{Language and Exploration State}
Language can represent input, output, or both. Sparklis uses controlled choices to verbalize an evolving query. Rhizomer describes filter state in natural language. ForestQB combines forms with a RASA intent/entity interface and example questions \citep{2017_Ferre_Sparklis_An,V3-01105,V3-01155}. These mechanisms do not imply unrestricted natural-language understanding. ForestQB's report describes particular wildlife tasks and query conversions; it does not establish arbitrary conversational access to any RDF endpoint.

\citet{V3-00696} combine topic classification, hierarchical paper clusters, and grounded comparisons of scholarly publications in a conversational interface over a Neo4j knowledge graph. Their forty-participant comparison counterbalances interface order between two groups, while scenario order remains fixed. Mean SUS scores for the conversational and graphical interfaces are 76.00 and 77.25 in the first scenario, and 76.63 and 65.25 in the second. The report presents descriptive ratings without a significance test or objective retrieval/time gain. Participants also reported difficulties with backtracking and with choosing between buttons and free text. The example connects generated guidance with a constrained task flow; it does not establish unrestricted conversation or a benefit across domains.

The medical exploration studies provide direct observations about guidance and orientation. \citet{2021_Witschel_Natural_Language-based} randomly assigned ten Medical Informatics students to two groups of five using the same prototype with or without query recommendations. Participants explored a patient case described by age, gender, and symptoms. The authors operationalized an insight as a task-relevant retrieved result coded from session videos: expected diagnostic information included possible diseases, differentiating symptoms, patient groups, and treatments, with additional relevant results also counted. Mean counts were 5.4 with recommendations and 3.4 without. The authors framed the small study as qualitative; these counts do not establish diagnostic accuracy or a general effectiveness gain. They also observed premature query selection and inadequate context. \citet{2022_Grether_Studying_Interaction} found different preferences for textual and visual navigation and reported the usefulness of clustering for orientation. The results support attention to context and recovery, with the populations and tasks limiting broader conclusions.

Exploration state includes the current entities, filters, query focus, source scope, and prior decisions. LODmilla's saved state and undo support revisiting a graph selection \citep{2014_AMA_LODmilla_Shared}. FaRBIE combines partial results with visible source, error, progress, and response information \citep{V3-01117}. These features can help users distinguish an empty result from an incomplete response and can preserve orientation as asynchronous operations finish. The available reports often describe the mechanism more fully than its tested effect, so the design argument remains separate from a comparative usability claim.

Guidance can reduce the visible choices while introducing a risk of losing the desired target. SView ranks link patterns by goodness and diversity and exposes hierarchy, list and top-$k$ organizations. Its user comparison found shorter interactions with top-$k$ patterns, but twelve of the twenty-four participants noted target-loss risk; hierarchy improved overview while deep branches confused some participants \citep{V3-01744}. RelClus instead groups relationship paths into a labeled hierarchy, with expansion and collapse for inspecting the grouped results \citep{V3-02740}. Ranked pruning and grouped overview support different actions. A system that reduces options should retain a route back to the full candidate set and distinguish an absent target from one hidden by ranking.

An implemented host interface does not make every guidance extension implemented. The OptiqueVQS query-history report specifies conditional-probability ranking of continuations through partial-query matching, illustrated with an assumed query log; the ranking is a proposed method. A later query-log report combines proposed ranking and dead-end approximation, reporting a preliminary elimination of up to 80\% of dead ends using views about 10\% of the original data \citep{V3-02739,V3-01984}. Combined ranking and dead-end evaluation remains future work. Separately, OWL graph projection supplies a concrete navigation component used in OptiqueVQS and SemFacet, while summarizing earlier application evaluations rather than recruiting a new cohort \citep{V3-01948}. These reports warrant separate method, implementation and study identities within the same family.

Conversational exploration also depends on a declared interaction scope. The news agent of Schneider et al. uses a Neo4j graph and structured exploration modes, with suggestions for related entities; unrestricted language-model generation is future work. Its 54 users produced 65 sessions, with mean SUS 79.4 (SD 18.5), but only 130 of 222 entity-related search requests were recognized \citep{V3-01173}. Seabra et al. implement clarification and reformulation with a knowledge graph and GPT-4o, illustrated through four underspecified questions about historical Brazilian capitals; quantitative participant evaluation remains future work \citep{V3-01170}. User ratings in the former and clarification examples in the latter provide different evidence. Neither establishes arbitrary conversational access across graphs or reliable resolution of every ambiguous question.

Preferences can affect filtering, ordering and subsequent recommendations in different ways. PFSgeo combines facets with rectangular geographic selection and best/worst preference actions; one geographical facet is implemented. SEED V3 applies weighted positive and negative entity/aspect feedback and provides graph-link explanations of embedding-ranked entities \citep{V3-02091,V3-02418}. LINDASearch records preference learning and query recommendations, but its three-expert assessment concerns analytic annotation choices rather than a three-person end-to-end search trial \citep{V3-01754}. An application should distinguish a hard restriction from a soft preference and retain the feedback history that changes a ranked result. A related graph path explains a connection without proving that an embedding score itself is a source assertion.

EmpiRE-Compass couples curated competency questions with custom natural-language querying, SPARQL inspection, manual or prompt-based refinement, and restoration of query/analysis history. Curated interpretations are currently written manually for correctness; custom interpretations use language-model generation and require validation \citep{V3-00942}. The two implemented research use cases support a concrete exploration and reuse workflow, while expert reviews, pilot studies and larger assessments remain planned. No chart type, linked brushing or measured cognitive benefit is inferred from the dashboard label. Eligibility and extraction concern the complete seven-page proceedings report, published on 2 June 2026, rather than treating its original one-page poster as a sufficient method report.

The Hippalus automatic-ranking extension makes relaxation and ordering inspectable. SmartFSRank retains preference buckets, fills a short result set with approximate objects and uses discrimination statistics to break large ranking ties; its object explanations expose the score components and satisfied preferences. Added objects can violate hard constraints, so their approximate status affects the interpretation of the result population \citep{V3-01079}. Computational simulations use each of 382 hotels as a target: at top-one retrieval, common-value ranking requires a mean 4.11 hard constraints, compared with 4.55 without automatic ranking, while rare-value ranking worsens this to 4.85. These are simulated target-finding costs. The separately reported action latency rises from about 2 to 20 milliseconds with ranking; neither result establishes a measured human-task improvement or a performance bound for much larger data.

\section{Backend Assumptions and Scalability}
\subsection{Model, Access, and Configuration}
Graph model, access route, query language, and storage engine are separate properties. An RDF graph can be explored by dereferencing documents, querying an endpoint, or loading a local index. A native property graph can expose typed entities and relationships through Cypher. A generic collection of labeled graphs can support subgraph search without the assumptions of either model. Recording an engine name alone does not establish the language used by an application. Similarly, parallel access to several sources does not prove that the interface performs federated joins.

Schema information ranges from source ontologies to application-maintained configurations and discovered instance patterns. The /facet browser computes schema and property closures around its repository \citep{2006_Hildebrand_facet_A}. Sparklis discovers endpoint structure without requiring prior schema preprocessing \citep{2017_Ferre_Sparklis_An}. ViziQuer uses schema and resource information for contextual completion; the DBpedia report describes indexed context and explicit query restrictions \citep{V3-01013}. Sampo-UI provides reusable components while still requiring domain perspectives and query configuration \citep{2022_Ikkala_Sampo-UI_A}. ``Generic'' can therefore mean portability of software, automatic discovery, or freedom from domain-specific design; these are different claims.

SemSpect reports distinct implementations for both RDF and labelled property graphs \citep{V3-02383}. Its RDF implementation uses compact indices built from dump snapshots, whereas its property-graph implementation uses Neo4j. Grouped exploration and query export demonstrate shared interaction concepts, but do not establish arbitrary SPARQL--Cypher equivalence or a controlled cross-backend usability advantage.

The manufacturing exploration report illustrates a hybrid architecture. It combines RDF statement-level provenance, scoped perspectives, recommendations, and several access components, including SPARQL and Gremlin \citep{V3-00949}. Its optional property-graph projection does not justify classifying every input as a native property graph, and a Cypher reference in background material is not evidence of an implemented Cypher interface. The distinction also matters for maintenance: projected facts, cached indexes, and ranked suggestions may refer to different data versions unless their update relationship is specified.

\subsection{Performance Evidence and Its Scope}
Scalability strategies include bounding neighborhood/path expansion, pagination, caching, aggregating results, summary indexes, and progressive updates. These mechanisms address different bottlenecks. LODmilla bounds graph traversal; rdf:SynopsViz organizes numeric and temporal data through hierarchical groups; ProLOD++ analyzes graph patterns and precomputes profiles \citep{2014_AMA_LODmilla_Shared,2014_Bikakis_rdfSynopsViz_a,V3-01058}. The ProLOD++ component-size threshold above 1,000 guides processing choices; it is not a demonstrated limit on the size of a visible graph.

Numeric evidence must retain its workload. The FAAS timing experiment covers 268 property-autocompletion requests: four join templates combined with 67 weighted-sampled Wikidata properties, completing the property variable \texttt{?q}. All FAAS requests completed within three seconds, while 36\% of WDQS requests completed within the endpoint timeout. The local index used the 23 May 2020 Wikidata truthy dump, filtered to 1.098 billion triples. Preprocessing took 75 hours and produced a 9.7-GB index on an i7-4600M CPU at 2.9 GHz with 16 GB RAM and Windows 10 \citep{2021_Parra_Fast_Approximate}. The timing workload covers all 268 requests; the reported median precision of 0.21 concerns only requests for which WDQS returned results. Neither quantity measures complete human task time. Graph-Query Suggestions reports average retrieval/query-answering below one second and millisecond scoring over a 76-million-node, 314-million-edge graph; these are component times, not complete human task durations \citep{2020_Lissandrini_Graph-Query_Suggestions}. FeedLens reports a twelve-fold speedup for a separate offline summary-embedding experiment, whose integration was future work in the assessed interface \citep{V3-01126}.

RDF Surveyor supplies live-operation averages with differences between deployment settings. Standalone repository loading averaged 2.42 seconds and global search 3.71 seconds, whereas DataGraft measurements were 1.65 and 0.41 seconds respectively \citep{V3-01765}. These tracked operations provide useful response evidence, but the settings are not matched randomized task conditions. The Oracle faceted-search report benchmarks materialization and filter operations on specified hardware: an initial 18-million-row result took 35 seconds, with bitmap and in-memory refinements varying by the number of substituted variables \citep{V3-01056}. The printed 38.00-second bitmap value is retained rather than silently corrected to fit a speed narrative.

Resource failure is also informative. LinkDaViz reports a browser-memory failure for a selected dataset above 300 million triples \citep{2015_Thellmann_LinkDaViz_Automatic}. Sampo-UI reports large deployments, including a portal with more than 241 million triples, but does not supply a controlled latency benchmark for that graph \citep{2022_Ikkala_Sampo-UI_A}. A stored graph size, rendered item count, preprocessing cost, request latency, and task duration should therefore be reported as separate quantities. The recovered sources expose distinct portability costs. Humboldt works with locally loaded RDF documents and SPARQL; run-time dereferencing remains a planned extension. Source size and interactive cost remain separate in the extension. La Rioja Turismo reports 67,284 entities and 675,368 triples in a deployed tourism graph; approximately 40,000 monthly visits are deployment traffic. Measured scalability can depend sharply on the action and cache state. SPARQL-QBE reports average query execution of 103 milliseconds on its approximately one-million-triple movie dataset; feedback ranking initially ranges from 103 to 824 milliseconds and is about 103 milliseconds when cached. These are client-component times on the specified browser and hardware \citep{V3-02185}. PFSgeo separately tests synthetic datasets up to 20,000 hotels: average initialization is 1.8 seconds, yet some large-data preference actions exceed 100 seconds and the per-object alternative times out \citep{V3-02091}. Its human trial uses only twenty hotels. Reporting an initialization average or a warm-cache result as a uniform interaction bound would conceal a material workload difference.

\section{Evaluation Evidence}
\subsection{What the Studies Measure}
Evaluation addresses several questions that should remain separate. A formal analysis can establish expressiveness or complexity. An offline benchmark can measure ranking quality, candidate precision, or query execution. A user study can examine formulation success, interpretation, workload, or experience. Deployment statistics describe uptake and use under operational conditions. None alone establishes all aspects of exploration quality. The comparison retains reports and studies as evidence units without constructing a single effectiveness score.

The gFacet 2008 report recruited ten people with differing prior knowledge of facets and used search tasks, eye tracking, and questionnaires \citep{2008_Heim_gFacet_A}. It did not compare two browsers. LinkDaViz evaluated seven tasks with twenty participants and examined difficulty, satisfaction, recommendation quality, and resource behavior without a user-study control \citep{2015_Thellmann_LinkDaViz_Automatic}. These reports provide evidence about discoverability and task experience, with different designs and outcomes. Their sample sizes do not make the results directly combinable.

\subsection{Comparators, Participants, and Outcomes}
In the FeedLens evaluation, fifteen participants performed two literature-review tasks in counterbalanced conditions, and ten supplied usable interaction logs \citep{V3-01126}. The System Usability Scale (SUS) mean was 84 for FeedLens and 77.3 for the Semantic Scholar baseline. Task duration was longer, 15.8 versus 10.76 minutes; the authors interpreted this as engagement. Increased interaction and longer use cannot simultaneously be treated as proof of faster task completion. The relevance analysis also includes recommender-derived scores, so it is not independent ground truth for every outcome.

The RDF Surveyor trial reports SUS 65 with standard deviation 16 across fourteen data scientists, with lower ratings among the least experienced participants \citep{V3-01765}. The experience subgroups were not randomized. Its separate tool-feasibility comparison and analytics do not turn this into a controlled comparative user study. Rhizomer reports six new participants and compares a task duration with an earlier formative version \citep{V3-01105}. The historical rounds used different participants, and the wording of the earlier and later tasks differs. The reported improvement is therefore retained as a historical comparison rather than a causal estimate from a matched experiment.

ViziQuer illustrates why report-level provenance matters. The 2017 study tested interpretation of ten static visual queries with six medical master's students and a teacher after an introduction \citep{V3-01077}. The 2022 DBpedia study tested query construction by ten IT master's students with prior experience \citep{V3-01013}. A separate conversion exercise in the latter used 150 QALD-9 queries; it is not a 150-person trial. The QueryVOWL demonstration and detailed notation report share a system and evaluation context and cannot be added as independent cohorts \citep{V3-02190,V3-02191}.

\subsection{Missingness and Appraisal}
Missing participant counts and incomplete statistical reporting limit synthesis. S-Paths reports nine sessions but persona counts sum to eight, while its questionnaire count does not align with the listed lay-user and reuser counts \citep{2021_Destandau_S-Paths_Set-based}. The discrepancy is retained rather than resolved through an invented sample. Sparklis logs identify 950 distinct IP addresses; these are not 950 known individuals or a controlled user trial \citep{2017_Ferre_Sparklis_An}. Graph-Query Suggestions reports more than 25,000 judgments with at least three judgments per pair, while the number of unique raters is unreported \citep{2020_Lissandrini_Graph-Query_Suggestions}.

The reports' contributions are bounded by these limitations. Qualitative observations can identify context-selection problems, unfamiliar controls, or differences in user strategies without estimating a population effect. Offline ranking can identify a dependence on query context without proving that users find more valuable discoveries. Deployment evidence can support operational feasibility without establishing causal usability gains. The resulting evidence is therefore synthesized by design and outcome, with no pooled participant total, standardized effect, or overall tool ranking.

The recovered comparisons extend the evidence without supplying a common effectiveness scale. RDF Explorer compares query construction with Wikidata Query Helper in a within-subject study of 28 fourth-year computer-science students without prior SPARQL knowledge. Reported differences favor RDF Explorer for whole-query correctness and graph shape, while the triple-pattern difference is not significant \citep{V3-02210}. Smeagol compares 20 users with 23 Pubby users; correctness differences are significant for more complex topologies but not the simple two-node tasks. Its text mentions twelve tasks while the results table lists ten \citep{V3-02525}. Task reach under fixed time and inconsistent task inventories remain part of the interpretation, rather than being removed when describing a favorable result.

The industrial OptiqueVQS report contributes two small domain trials: three Statoil experts and four Siemens experts. Correct completion is reported as 84\% and 88\%, respectively, with first-attempt completion of 69\% and 72\%; the report also summarizes an earlier fifteen-student movie study \citep{V3-02014}. The earlier group is not a newly recruited industrial cohort. In SView, the browser and pattern-organization comparisons share the same twenty-four-student study setting; mean SUS is 79.8 for SView, 70.32 for Rhizomer and 64.51 for OpenLink, while reorientation and link-label ratings expose contrary findings \citep{V3-01744}. Neither repeating the shared sample across analyses nor adding prior-study summaries creates independent participants.

The PFSgeo and SPARQL-QBE evaluations require task-response denominators. Twenty PFSgeo users contribute eighty responses per interface, with 79/80 successful responses using the map and 63/80 without it. Interface order is fixed: without map precedes map use. The source reports usefulness categories rather than a standard ten-item SUS score \citep{V3-02091}. SPARQL-QBE recruits twenty-two computer-science volunteers for ten tasks, yielding 220 responses with 65 failures; favorable usefulness ratings coexist with difficulty translating some tasks into actions \citep{V3-02185}. These findings motivate evaluation of recovery and query understanding, but do not imply 160 or 220 users or causal superiority across interfaces.

Questionnaire and response units also need reconciliation within a report. FedViz reports fifteen SUS respondents and ten respondents to a custom questionnaire, but their overlap is unestablished; they cannot be summed as twenty-five unique participants \citep{V3-01125}. The Sparklis analytical-query study describes twelve pairs while its analysis also uses twelve subjects. Its reported mean answered-question counts, 1.67 with Yasgui and 6.17 with Sparklis, belong to those recorded response units; a unique individual count is not inferred \citep{V3-00369}. The Sparklis extension, RDF Explorer demonstration/research/Android reports, ViziQuer reports, and OptiqueVQS components retain family links. Shared names or repeated results prompt an overlap check rather than an automatic merge or an independent-study count.

Offline guidance tests answer different questions from human trials. iQbees evaluates 163 INEX topics with simulated feedback: positive and mixed feedback improve its ranking, while a single negative hint does not. Von-QBE evaluates selected IMDB and QALD queries without recruiting users; reported precision is 0.69 for IMDB and 0.0047 for the QALD subset \citep{V3-01566,V3-02018}. The ViziQuer Wikidata demonstration successfully visualizes 105 of 120 example queries under a criterion combining returned-result agreement with mathematical equivalence for timeout or empty cases \citep{V3-02757}. Ranking quality, keyword-to-query precision and query round-trip coverage are distinct outcomes. They do not establish human task success or comprehension, even when embedded in an implemented exploration workflow.

\section{Discussion and Design Agenda}
\subsection{Task Fit and Progressive Control}
RQ1 is answered through the relationships between tasks and operations. Navigation supports inspection from a known entity; facets support repeated narrowing of an identifiable population; path discovery makes connections the object of search; query construction makes relationships and logical constraints explicit. Coordinated views support aggregate interpretation when their selection state is preserved. A future application can combine these operations, but switching should retain the user's entities, constraints, and source scope. The representative reports support this requirement more directly than a preference for one dominant visual style.

User expertise should guide the amount and timing of control. A domain specialist may know which entities matter while being unfamiliar with query joins; a technical user may understand SPARQL while lacking domain vocabulary. The medical studies and RDF Surveyor experience differences show that these dimensions should be measured separately \citep{2021_Witschel_Natural_Language-based,2022_Grether_Studying_Interaction,V3-01765}. Progressive controls can begin with meaningful examples and valid refinements, then expose relationship direction, query scope, and advanced constructs. This is a design implication from observed problems and supported mechanisms, not an established cross-system effect size.

\subsection{Guidance That Remains Inspectable}
RQ2 concerns the contract between a suggestion and the action it invites. An application should state whether a candidate is schema-compatible, observed in data, approximate, or ranked by relevance. It should distinguish a guaranteed executable step from a potentially useful extension. The low precision of approximate autocompletion and the weak single-exemplar ranking result make this distinction concrete \citep{2021_Parra_Fast_Approximate,2020_Lissandrini_Graph-Query_Suggestions}. Users need the ability to inspect the reason for a suggestion and undo its effect without losing their prior state.

Explanation also concerns the displayed knowledge. Source assertions, inferred facts, similarity edges, and manually defined domain connections should carry different labels and provenance. Confidence values require an account of their origin and aggregation assumptions. The Whyis Knowledge Explorer describes confidence-weighted graph views and on-demand expansion, but its example confidence settings do not establish calibrated probabilities \citep{V3-00740}. Personalization controls should expose the active preference context and its effect on ranked entities, as the explicit topic/category controls in Grapevine2D illustrate \citep{V3-00694}.

\subsection{Bounded Computation and Reproducible Evaluation}
RQ3 links portability with computation. A general endpoint interface must handle incomplete schema, slow queries, partial responses, and unavailable sources. A local index improves control of latency but introduces preprocessing and freshness costs. A domain portal can offer clear concepts through curated configuration while requiring ongoing maintenance. An application should expose bounds, sampling, pagination, and update dates at the point where they affect interpretation. An empty display should reveal whether it means no match, an applied bound, a timeout, or an unavailable source.

For RQ4, the reported evidence supports different, bounded conclusions. Usability ratings and qualitative observations describe experience in particular tasks and samples. Comparative studies can reveal tradeoffs: FeedLens reports a higher mean SUS score and longer task duration than its baseline, while RDF Surveyor reports experience-dependent ratings without a randomized comparator \citep{V3-01126,V3-01765}. Query-interpretation and construction exercises, offline ranking, and execution benchmarks measure different outcomes; their results do not establish a common cross-system effectiveness estimate. Future trials should state participant expertise, domain familiarity, training, task materials, allocation, task order, and comparator version. Correctness and time can be combined with qualitative observation when their definitions are clear. Discovery tasks also need criteria for the usefulness, novelty, and evidential support of findings. Component benchmarks should separate network retrieval, ranking, query execution, transfer, and rendering, with data size and hardware. A large source graph is not sufficient evidence of scalable interaction.

For RQ5, the assessed reports describe different dependencies on configuration, preprocessing, and versions. Sampo-UI requires domain perspectives and query configuration; the approximate-autocompletion method requires a preprocessed summary index; ViziQuer constructs depend on the assessed version \citep{2022_Ikkala_Sampo-UI_A,2021_Parra_Fast_Approximate,2018_Cerans_ViziQuer_A,V3-01013}. These dependencies limit direct reuse unless the source data, configuration, and version are retained. A source-reported repository or demonstration address is an artifact lead, not a current availability check. A reachable article is not source code, and a repository for a conversion pipeline may not contain the interface. The assessed version, license, release or commit, test data, configuration, tasks, and expected results should be identified when available. Later artifacts must not be backdated to an earlier report. These distinctions allow engineering reuse without overstating what the review has verified.

Reproducibility in the extension also depends on preserving configuration and the assessed implementation. KinGVisher permits sharing graph configurations and reports code and demonstration addresses; its maximum-edge setting and property selection affect what the shared view contains. The schema library reports cached JSON schema descriptions and a schema-library repository, while SPARQL-QBE loads RDF text files into a custom browser-side executor \citep{V3-01624,V3-00138,V3-02185}. Reusing a graph image, a schema diagram or a generated query alone can therefore omit the bounds, data snapshot or executor subset needed to reproduce the result. The reported resource addresses are artifact leads; their presence does not establish a current release, license, archived version or successful independent reproduction.

\section{Limitations}
The review is bounded by its frozen sources, English-language criterion, working protocol, and documented retrieval state. At the historical baseline, 324 of 991 sought reports were assessed and 667 were not retrieved. The current extension raises assessment to 437/991 (44.10\%); 543/991 (54.79\%) remain not retrieved and 11/991 (1.11\%) await assessment. This large retrieval gap limits coverage and prevents a claim that the assessed reports represent the full eligible field. The existing collection has incomplete original-discovery provenance. Planned searches without execution evidence are not counted. Original ACM, OpenAlex, IEEE, and supplementary Google Scholar results exports are absent, and the exact submitted IEEE block strings were not retained, limiting independent search reproduction. Database indexing can omit reports or produce divergent dates and titles. Publication-track verification can remain unresolved even when substantive content is readable. These limitations affect coverage and selection and do not establish limitations of an underlying exploration method.

The descriptive profile covers the included, retrieved reports. The narrative comparisons use illustrative, source-located examples and do not estimate feature prevalence across all eligible systems. Reports often omit participant counts, latency conditions, task materials, or stable artifact versions. Qualitative studies and domain deployments provide context-dependent evidence. Historical full-text eligibility was reviewed by one person; three people participated in extraction, but independently duplicated extraction and per-report checking are not documented. The recovery extension uses scoped Codex extraction and author-authorized criteria application without additional human verification or blinded duplicate screening. Its source locators make the revised claims inspectable, but do not establish complete reading or eliminate extraction error. The synthesis makes no pooled effectiveness or certainty-of-evidence claim. Separate reports about a system may still contain unrecognized shared data; where sharing is not verified, no independent study count is inferred.

\section{Conclusions}
Knowledge graph exploration involves choices about actions, suggestions, views, data access, computation, and evidence. Comparing those choices reveals why similar interfaces can support different tasks and why strong component results do not automatically establish end-to-end usability. The six-dimensional framework preserves these distinctions and connects concrete report findings with application requirements. The most defensible design implications are to preserve interpretable state, explain the scope and basis of guidance, expose retrieval and computation limits, and evaluate claims under explicit task and user conditions. The current descriptive profile covers 315 included reports, comprising the historical 242-report baseline and 73 recovered primary reports. The extension strengthens comparisons of schema/query/result roles, example feedback, conversational scope and workload-specific performance, while its machine-assisted extraction provenance remains distinct from historical human review. The illustrative comparisons explain mechanisms and source-specific evidence. The frozen search and retrieval gaps limit extension of these findings to the full eligible field.

\section{Operational Protocol Summary}\label{app:protocol}
The core inclusion test requires both a semantic graph exploration object and a concrete interaction or guidance contribution. Evaluation is desirable but is not mandatory for eligibility. Code and demonstration availability are extraction fields rather than inclusion criteria. An absent abstract or inaccessible full text is not a substantive exclusion by itself. Matching title--abstract Exclude votes close that stage; matching Include or Maybe votes advance to full text. Missing or invalid votes and disagreements require a separately recorded routing decision based on the criteria, preserving the original votes. All 185 unresolved title--abstract cases were advanced on 6 October 2026 without imputing missing votes.

Exclusions distinguish topic failures, non-English reports, non-primary genre, insufficient report detail, and failures to establish publication status. A construction, ontology-engineering, isolated-answer, or backend report can meet scope only if it supplies a primary exploration or identifiable guidance contribution. A proposed method is not excluded solely because a graphical interface has not been implemented. Final eligibility reasons remain attached to individual reports rather than assigned from a system name or keyword.

\section{Exclusion and Retrieval Boundaries}\label{app:exclusions}
The exclusion register distinguishes a report's disposition from the absence of accessible evidence. A tutorial about an existing interface remains tutorial material even when it describes useful functions. An unreviewed preprint can contain detailed methods and results while failing the publication criterion. A foreign-language body is not made English by an English abstract. A supplement is not a complete copy of its main report. A site error or missing editorial statement does not prove that a method fails the exploration criterion. These distinctions are retained in the report-level register with the criterion and source.

Sapphire remains a historical bibliographic reference with its recorded title--abstract exclusion disposition \citep{2016_El-Roby_Sapphire_Querying}. A substantive read profile does not overwrite that disposition or create an included study. Contextual reviews likewise appear in the reference list without entering the primary-system denominator. The complete exclusion register and final report disposition accompany the review workbook.
